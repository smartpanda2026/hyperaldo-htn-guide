Secondary Hypertension

A practical, integrated reference for the diagnosis and treatment of primary aldosteronism and other secondary causes of hypertension — combining AHA/ACC 2025, Endocrine Society 2025, BIHS 2023/2024, and JAMA IM 2026 guidance.

⚠️ Educational reference only. This tool summarizes published guidelines for clinician reference. It is not a substitute for clinical judgment, institutional protocol, or the full guideline documents. Assay-specific cutoffs must be verified against your local laboratory.

Why it matters

Secondary causes are present in ~5–25% of adults with hypertension. Primary aldosteronism is the most common identifiable cause — and it is potentially curable (unilateral disease) or specifically targetable (bilateral disease with MRAs).

The four key sources

AHA/ACC 2025 — targeted case detection with Class 1 indications.
Endocrine Society 2025 — universal ARR screening position.
BIHS 2023/2024 — UK practical approach addressing local variation.
JAMA IM 2026 — critical review questioning ARR and suppression testing reliance.

The core triad (PA)

Diagnosis rests on demonstrating suppressed renin + inappropriately elevated aldosterone + an elevated ARR — then confirming, then lateralizing.

Key insight

Suppressed renin despite RAAS inhibitors or diuretics is itself strongly suggestive of PA. The combined pattern matters more than any single value.

AHA/ACC 2025 — Screen These Adults Class 1

Screen adults with hypertension if any of the following are present:

  • Resistant hypertension — regardless of whether hypokalemia is present
  • Hypokalemia — spontaneous or diuretic-induced
  • Obstructive sleep apnea
  • Incidentally discovered adrenal mass
  • Family history of early-onset hypertension
  • Stroke at a young age (<40 years)

May also consider Class 2b in adults with stage 2 hypertension.

General Suspicion Triggers for Any Secondary Cause

  • Stage 2 hypertension
  • Treatment-resistant hypertension
  • Sudden-onset hypertension
  • Loss of previously good control
  • Early-onset hypertension

A careful medication/substance history is essential — prescription, OTC, herbal, and illicit agents can all raise BP.

Endocrine Society 2025 — The Broader Position Conditional

The updated Endocrine Society Clinical Practice Guideline goes further than AHA/ACC, suggesting screening all individuals with hypertension — a move toward universal case detection. This reflects evidence that PA is far more prevalent than previously recognized and that hypokalemia is absent in the majority of cases.

BIHS 2023/2024 — The UK Pragmatic View Consensus

The BIHS document acknowledges that UK National guidelines do not provide extensive PA recommendations, and local practice varies. Its aim is to raise awareness, guide screening/diagnosis, and support treatment to mitigate adverse cardiometabolic effects in otherwise undiagnosed individuals. The BIHS emphasizes that PA remains often unrecognized, particularly in resistant hypertension.

Medication Management

✅ Continue most antihypertensives

The AHA/ACC guideline advises continuing most antihypertensives during initial screening — including ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and CCBs.

⚠️ Beta-blockers and central alpha-agonists

These suppress renin and may cause false positives. Hold for 2 weeks if clinically safe.

Withdraw for at least 4 weeks before testing. They reliably produce a false negative.

1. Timing & Posture — Get This Right First

⏰ Timing
  • Morning draw — ideally between 8:00 and 10:00 AM
  • Aldosterone and renin both follow a circadian rhythm
  • Avoid late-afternoon draws
  • Ensure the patient has been up and about before the draw
🪑 Posture
  • Seated for 5–15 minutes before venipuncture
  • Same posture for both aldosterone and renin sampling
  • Avoid prolonged recumbency — it suppresses renin
  • Ambulation before the draw is acceptable and preferred

2. Potassium — The Most Common False-Negative Cause

🧂 Potassium rules
  • Correct hypokalemia before testing. Hypokalemia falsely lowers aldosterone.
  • Measure potassium alongside aldosterone — interpret together.
  • If the screen is negative in a hypokalemic patient, correct potassium and repeat.
  • Potassium supplements and potassium-sparing diuretics can normalize K⁺ without invalidating the ARR.

3. Sodium Intake — Do Not Restrict

🧂 Sodium rules
  • Unrestricted, liberal salt intake for at least 3 days before testing.
  • Sodium restriction raises renin and aldosterone — obscuring the pattern of PA.
  • No need to formally sodium-load for the initial screen.
  • 24-hour urinary sodium can confirm intake if the result is ambiguous.

4. Drug Holds — The Practical Cheat Sheet

🛑 HOLD before testing (when clinically safe)
  • Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists — the most important hold.
    • Spironolactone: 4–6 weeks
    • Eplerenone: 2–4 weeks
  • Beta-blockers — suppress renin. Hold 2 weeks if BP allows.
  • Central alpha-agonists — clonidine, methyldopa. Hold 2 weeks if safe; taper to avoid rebound.
  • NSAIDs — can raise potassium and blunt renin. Hold 1–2 weeks if possible.
  • Licorice, carbenoxolone, chewing tobacco — stop 2 weeks before.
✅ CONTINUE during testing (per AHA/ACC 2025)
  • ACE inhibitors / ARBs — continue. A suppressed renin on these is strongly suggestive of PA.
  • Calcium channel blockers — continue. Non-DHP (verapamil, diltiazem) preferred for substitutions.
  • Thiazide / loop diuretics — may be continued if needed for BP.
  • Alpha-blockers (doxazosin, prazosin, terazosin) — safe, non-interfering.
  • Hydralazine — safe, non-interfering.
🔄 If a hold is not safe — substitute instead
  • Replace interfering agents with non-interfering agents for 2–4 weeks, then repeat the ARR.
  • Preferred substitutions: verapamil or diltiazem, doxazosin, hydralazine.
  • Avoid substituting with agents that themselves interfere.

5. Sample Collection & Handling

🧪 Tube & handling
  • Collect in EDTA or heparin tube — follow your local lab's validated protocol.
  • Renin sample on ice — place the tube in an ice slurry immediately after draw.
  • Centrifuge cold (4°C) and separate plasma promptly.
  • Freeze plasma at −20°C or below if not assayed immediately.
🧊 Common handling mistakes
  • Renin sample left at room temperature → falsely low PRA
  • Delayed centrifugation → cryoactivation → falsely high PRA
  • Hemolyzed sample → unreliable potassium
  • Wrong tube type (e.g., serum separator for renin)

6. The Pre-Test Checklist — Print This

  • Patient off MRAs for ≥4 weeks (spironolactone) or ≥2 weeks (eplerenone), unless unsafe
  • Potassium normal — corrected if hypokalemic, and measured with the sample
  • Sodium intake liberal — not restricted for at least 3 days
  • Beta-blockers / central alpha-agonists held for 2 weeks if clinically safe
  • NSAIDs, licorice, carbenoxolone stopped for 1–2 weeks
  • Morning draw — 8:00–10:00 AM, patient ambulatory beforehand
  • Seated 5–15 minutes before venipuncture, same posture for both assays
  • Renin sample on ice, centrifuged cold, plasma separated promptly
  • Both aldosterone and renin sent to the lab with the correct tube types
  • Local lab cutoffs confirmed — assay type and units known
  • If result borderline or negative with high suspicion — plan a repeat after drug substitution or potassium correction

7. Common Pitfalls — Why Screens Fail

⚠️ Top false-negative causes
  • Hypokalemia at sampling → falsely low aldosterone
  • MRAs not held → receptor blocked, renin raised, aldosterone lowered
  • Sodium restriction → renin and aldosterone both raised, ARR misleading
  • Afternoon or recumbent draw → non-diagnostic values
  • Wrong assay interpretation → using an immunoassay cutoff on LC-MS/MS
  • Renin sample mishandled → falsely low PRA → falsely high ARR
⚠️ Top false-positive causes
  • Beta-blockers or central alpha-agonists → renin suppressed without PA
  • Advanced age or CKD → low renin is common
  • High aldosterone from another cause (e.g., heart failure, cirrhosis)
  • Renin cryoactivation → falsely high PRA (uncommon with proper handling)
💡 The bottom line: A correctly collected sample — morning, seated, liberal sodium, normal potassium, MRAs held — is worth more than any sophisticated assay.

Step 1 — Refer Class 1

A positive screen — or continued suspicion — warrants referral to a hypertension specialist or endocrinologist.

Step 2 — Confirm with Aldosterone Suppression

Confirmation typically uses an aldosterone suppression test — either IV saline suppression or oral salt-loading. The physiologic principle: in normal physiology, volume expansion suppresses aldosterone; in PA, aldosterone secretion is autonomous and fails to suppress.

IV Saline Suppression Test (SIT)

The most widely used confirmatory test. 2 L of 0.9% saline infused IV over 4 hours in the recumbent position, with aldosterone measured at baseline and at completion.

Interpretation: Failure of aldosterone to suppress below the protocol threshold is consistent with PA. Most commonly aldosterone >10 ng/dL; lower with LC-MS/MS.

Oral Salt Loading Test

High-sodium diet for 3 days with sodium supplementation, followed by measurement of 24-hour urinary aldosterone. Adequate sodium loading is confirmed by 24-hour urinary sodium measurement.

Interpretation: Urinary aldosterone excretion that fails to suppress is consistent with PA.

Captopril Challenge Test (CCT)

Oral captopril administered with aldosterone and renin measured before and after. In normal physiology, ACE inhibition lowers aldosterone; in PA, aldosterone remains elevated and the ARR does not normalize.

Simpler and safer than SIT in patients at risk of volume overload — but considered somewhat less definitive.

Fludrocortisone Suppression Test (FST)

Used predominantly in Australia and some European centers. Fludrocortisone plus sodium loading over several days, with serial aldosterone measurement. Considered highly sensitive but labor-intensive and requires monitoring for hypokalemia and volume overload.

The Evolving View: Is Confirmatory Testing Still Needed?

⚠️ Some experts and the JAMA Internal Medicine review argue the role of aldosterone suppression testing is uncertain. Reasons cited:
  • High false-negative rates — patients with genuine PA may suppress on testing and be incorrectly excluded
  • Emphasis should instead be placed on interpreting the individual renin and aldosterone values
  • Suppressed renin despite RAAS inhibitors or diuretics is itself strongly suggestive of PA

Where this leaves the clinician

The AHA/ACC and Endocrine Society guidelines still describe confirmatory testing as part of the standard pathway. The JAMA review does not advocate abandoning it, but argues it should not be treated as an absolute gatekeeper — particularly when the biochemical picture is unambiguous or when the patient is not a surgical candidate anyway.

Also note: a strongly positive screen should prompt adrenal CT — regardless of whether confirmatory testing is pursued.

🎯 The central rule: Cross-sectional imaging cannot determine functional activity. If the patient may be a surgical candidate, AVS is required regardless of what CT shows.

Why CT Alone Is Insufficient

  • CT identifies anatomy, not function
  • Nonfunctioning adrenal incidentalomas are common, especially with age
  • Bilateral hyperplasia can appear asymmetric on imaging
  • CT–AVS discordance is frequent

When AVS May Be Bypassed — The Recognized Exception

Endocrine Society recognized exception: AVS may be bypassed in a patient <35 years with:
  • Marked PA (unambiguous biochemistry)
  • Hypokalemia
  • A >1 cm unilateral adenoma on CT

Other situations where AVS may be skipped JAMA review

  • Patient is not interested in or not a candidate for surgery
  • Only a mildly positive ARR

In these cases, the result would not change management — the patient will receive medical therapy either way.

Adrenal Imaging — Always Obtain CT

CT should be obtained even if medical treatment is planned — to exclude a large mass (>4 cm or with suspicious features) that could represent adrenocortical carcinoma.

Adrenal Venous Sampling — The Protocol

AVS is the gold standard for lateralization. Adrenal vein blood is sampled bilaterally and compared with a peripheral (usually IVC) sample, with cortisol used as the normalizing hormone.

Interpreting AVS — The Three Indices

Selectivity Index (SI)

Purpose: Confirms the catheter actually reached the adrenal vein.

SI = adrenal vein cortisol ÷ peripheral cortisol

Typical thresholds: ≥2 unstimulated, ≥3 with ACTH stimulation.

Lateralization Index (LI)

Purpose: Determines whether aldosterone excess is unilateral or bilateral.

LI = (A/C)dominant ÷ (A/C)non-dominant

Typical threshold: ≥4 unstimulated suggests unilateral disease. With ACTH, ≥2 to ≥4 depending on protocol.

Contralateral Suppression Index (CSI)

Purpose: Supports unilateral disease by showing the opposite gland is suppressed.

CSI = (A/C)non-dominant ÷ (A/C)peripheral

A value <1 indicates suppression of the contralateral gland.

Why Thresholds Vary

LI and SI thresholds differ substantially between protocols. A single universal cutoff does not exist.

Post-Adrenalectomy Considerations

  • Anticipate resolution of hypokalemia — potassium supplementation usually stops quickly.
  • Transient hyperkalemia may occur as the suppressed contralateral gland recovers.
  • Blood pressure response is variable — predictors: duration of HTN, number of preoperative antihypertensives.
  • Continue to monitor — biochemical cure is more reliable than BP cure.

When to Suspect

  • Paroxysmal hypertension with the classic triad of headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis
  • Adrenal mass discovered incidentally — all adrenal incidentalomas should be screened for pheo
  • Resistant hypertension
  • Young-onset hypertension

Biochemical Diagnosis

First-line screening tests NCCN

  • Plasma free metanephrines — sensitivity ~97%, specificity ~93%. Higher sensitivity than urinary metanephrines for small or asymptomatic tumors.
  • 24-hour urine fractionated total metanephrines and catecholamines — alternative, widely available.
  • Elevations ≥3× the upper limit of normal are diagnostic.

Critical pre-analytical conditions

⚠️ Plasma metanephrines — draw conditions
  • Fully supine position, after ~30 minutes of rest
  • Fasting — avoid caffeine, nicotine, alcohol for 24 h
  • Avoid smoking — nicotine raises catecholamines
  • Review interfering drugs — tricyclics, antipsychotics, SSRIs/SNRIs, levodopa, MAO inhibitors, decongestants, acetaminophen
  • Do not draw during a hypertensive paroxysm — wait for baseline

Borderline elevations

Borderline elevations are common and require careful interpretation. Repeat testing under optimized conditions (supine, no interfering drugs) is usually the first step. Clonidine suppression testing can help distinguish true pheo from false-positive elevations, though it is less commonly used now that plasma free metanephrines are widely available.

Cervical paraganglioma

For cervical PGL, serum and/or 24-h urine fractionated catecholamines (particularly for dopamine) or methoxytyramine (where available) may be appropriate, since these tumors are more likely to be dopamine-secreting.

Genetic Testing

Universal germline testing is now recommended for all patients with PCC/PGL. Approximately 30–40% of PPGL are hereditary, involving >20 susceptibility genes (SDHB/C/D, VHL, RET, NF1, MAX, TMEM127, FH, and others). Restricting testing to SDHB/C/D alone misses roughly one-third of pathogenic variants. Multigene panel testing yields a pathogenic/likely pathogenic variant in ~27.5%.

Why universal testing matters

  • SDHB variants carry a 30–50% metastatic risk — identification changes surveillance and family screening
  • Hereditary PCC/PGL, MEN2, and VHL have appreciable risk of bilateral tumors — cortical-sparing adrenalectomy should be considered
  • Family testing depends on identifying the index variant
  • Surveillance protocols differ by genotype — SDHB requires more intensive imaging

Confirmatory / Differential Testing

High-dose dexamethasone suppression test (HDDST)

8 mg dexamethasone administered; cortisol measured. Suppression (>50% reduction) suggests Cushing disease; failure to suppress suggests ectopic ACTH.

CRH stimulation test

Ovine or human CRH administered IV; ACTH and cortisol measured. Exaggerated response suggests Cushing disease; blunted response suggests ectopic ACTH.

Bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS)

Gold standard for distinguishing pituitary from ectopic ACTH when imaging is equivocal.

Pituitary MRI

Thin-section (1–2 mm) with gadolinium. Identifies pituitary adenoma in ~60% of Cushing disease.

Two common, easily screened contributors to hypertension.

Thyroid Disease

When to suspect

  • Hyperthyroidism — weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor, palpitations
  • Hypothyroidism — fatigue, cold intolerance, diastolic hypertension
  • Atrial fibrillation — always check TSH

Screening test AHA/ACC

TSH and free T4.

Confirmatory

Radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU) for hyperthyroidism.

Management

  • Hyperthyroidism — antithyroid drugs, RAI, surgery
  • Hypothyroidism — levothyroxine, titrated to TSH

When to suspect

  • Daytime somnolence — Epworth Sleepiness Scale
  • Loud snoring reported by partner
  • Witnessed apneas
  • Resistant hypertension
  • Non-dipping nocturnal BP
  • Note: OSA is also a Class 1 screening indication for PA itself

Screening test AHA/ACC

STOP-BANG, Epworth questionnaire, and/or home polygraphy.

Confirmatory

Polysomnography — gold standard.

Management

  • CPAP — first-line for moderate-to-severe OSA
  • Mandibular advancement devices — mild-moderate OSA or CPAP intolerance
  • Weight loss — often most effective long-term

🧪 Aldosterone

Points of Universal Agreement

  • Suppressed renin is the hallmark of PA
  • Hypokalemia is not required for the diagnosis
  • Cross-sectional imaging cannot determine functional activity
  • AVS is required before surgery in most patients
  • Adrenal imaging must be obtained even if medical therapy is planned
  • Lateralization determines treatment — unilateral → adrenalectomy; bilateral → MRA
  • PA is an independent cardiovascular risk factor
